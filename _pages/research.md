### Should I Mail or Should I Go: Voting Behavior After a One-Time All-Postal Election
{: .research-title}

*With [Valentin Lindlacher](https://www.lindlacher.com/)* · [CESifo Working Paper No. 12075](https://doi.org/10.65864/5v2jtt32jl) (2026)
{: .research-meta}

**Abstract.** Can a one-time change in election administration affect voting behavior after regular rules return? During Bavaria's 2020 mayoral runoffs, postal voting was mandatory and ballots were automatically mailed to eligible voters. Subsequent elections returned to the usual voting rules. Using municipality-level election data from 2013 to 2025 and an event-study difference-in-differences design, we find a temporary increase in total turnout of approximately 0.4 percentage points and a persistent shift of about one percentage point from in-person to postal voting, lasting at least five years. We also document temporary changes in party support, including gains for the CSU. The results distinguish short-lived changes in overall participation from more durable changes in voting-mode choice.
{: .research-abstract}

</article>

### After the Electoral Breakdown: Voting and Precinct Organization in Berlin
{: .research-title}

*Single-authored* · [Latest version (PDF)](/files/Kroeper_AfterBreakdown.pdf)
{: .research-meta}

**Abstract.** How does voting change after an electoral breakdown? Using court-documented exposure and Berlin election returns from 2014 to 2026, I compare turnout in court-linked and clean-control precincts. Court-linked areas exhibit persistently lower in-person turnout and higher postal turnout in the 2024 European and 2025 federal elections. Between the 2017 and 2025 federal elections, treated-control differences change by -1.77 percentage points in person and +1.25 by post; the total-turnout estimate is smaller and imprecise (-0.52 points). Pre-breakdown changes between the 2014 and 2019 European elections were substantially smaller. Complementary geographic evidence shows a steeper association between baseline precinct electorate size and subsequent catchment reorganization in court-linked areas. The findings distinguish persistent voting-mode differences from changes in electoral administration.
{: .research-abstract}

</article>

### When Votes Are Already Fixed: Electoral Reopening and Voter Turnout
{: .research-title}

*With [Valentin Lindlacher](https://www.lindlacher.com/)* · [Latest version (PDF)](/files/Kroeper_Lindlacher_BerlinFixedVotes.pdf)
{: .research-meta}

**Abstract.** Does turnout differ when part of an election result is already legally fixed? Berlin's 2024 partial repeat of the 2021 federal election allowed voters in selected districts to replace their ballots, while valid votes elsewhere remained in the tally. We reconstruct retained first-vote totals and examine turnout in 294 repeat-affected reporting areas across twelve constituencies. Turnout declined less where a larger share of the constituency electorate could vote again. A one-percentage-point higher repeat share is associated with a 0.055-percentage-point smaller turnout decline, or 0.039 points after adjusting for an extrapolated historical differential trend. The geographic allocation of the repeat and earlier turnout trajectories limit causal interpretation. The findings concern the legally revisable scope of an election, not directly observed beliefs about pivotality.
{: .research-abstract}

</article>

### School Reassignment and Political Adaptation
{: .research-title}

*Single-authored · Work in progress*
{: .research-meta}

**Abstract.** This project examines whether residents' political affiliations adapt to school communities to which they become institutionally connected, even without residential moves. I reconstruct public-school reassignment events in North Carolina and link historical school-attendance boundaries to longitudinal voter-registration records, geocoded residences, and neighborhood characteristics. The design compares reassigned residents with residents remaining in the original school community, relating subsequent party-registration changes to the destination community's prior political orientation. Results suggest directional adaptation in adult party registration. Endogenous boundary changes, limited district-level variation, and incomplete validation of reassignment events mean the evidence does not yet establish a causal effect. The project investigates how institutional ties may shape political geography independently of residential mobility.
{: .research-abstract}

</article>

### A Year of College, a Lifetime of Party? Evidence from North Carolina Voter Registrations
{: .research-title}

*With [Sarah Listabarth](https://sarahlistabarth.github.io/) · Work in progress*
{: .research-meta}

**Abstract.** We examine how time spent in a college environment before a student's first general election relates to initial party affiliation. Using repeated North Carolina voter-registration records from 2008 to 2026, we identify likely college students from address histories and infer differences in the time spent at college before their first general election. We compare students with longer and shorter predicted exposure, controlling for their home areas and using inferred family connections for additional comparisons. In the main specifications, longer exposure is associated with a 1.2–1.4-percentage-point higher probability of Democratic registration, with some persistence into later elections. The study investigates political socialization during the transition into the electorate, while recognizing the limitations of inferred college attendance and nonrandom exposure.
{: .research-abstract}

</article>

### The Politics of Parental Protection: Sex Offender Proximity and Political Preferences
{: .research-title}

*With [Sarah Listabarth](https://sarahlistabarth.github.io/) · Preliminary research*
{: .research-meta}

**Abstract.** This project investigates whether exposure to nearby registered sex offenders affects parents' political preferences. We examine whether responses differ between mothers and fathers and by the gender of their children. More broadly, the project explores how parental concerns about family safety and local social environments translate into political behavior.
{: .research-abstract}

</article>

<article class="research-paper" markdown="1">

### The Destination Side of Migration: Evidence from Hurricanes
{: .research-title}

*Single-authored · Preliminary research*
{: .research-meta}

**Abstract.** Hurricane damage may influence migration not only by displacing residents of affected areas but also by disrupting potential destinations for people living elsewhere. Using longitudinal North Carolina voter-registration data, historical migration flows, and inundation from Hurricanes Matthew and Florence, I investigate how damage to historically important destinations affects migration from unaffected origins. Preliminary evidence suggests fewer moves from origins whose typical destinations experienced greater exposure, particularly for shorter-distance moves. I also examine whether these disruptions change who moves and the socioeconomic and political characteristics of the neighborhoods migrants select into. These latter analyses are descriptive rather than causal estimates of neighborhood effects.
{: .research-abstract}

</article>
